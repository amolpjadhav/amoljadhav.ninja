# Regression to the Mean: Why the Best Get Worse and the Worst Get Better

Category: Mental Models

---

Pick out anything that sits at an absolute extreme—the best month, the worst
quarter, the team on top of the table, the happiest week of your life—and the
next time you measure it, it will almost certainly be closer to ordinary. Not
because anything went wrong, but simply because extremes do not hold still.

Statisticians call this regression to the mean. The reason it tricks us so
consistently is that when something moves back toward normal, human beings
automatically look for what pushed it. We want a narrative. And there is usually
a convincing candidate standing right there, ready to take the credit or the
blame.

Just ask the flight instructors of the 1960s Israeli Air Force.

When a young psychologist named Daniel Kahneman stood in front of them and
explained that research proved praise worked better than punishment, the
instructors pushed back. They had years of hard evidence to the contrary. Praise
a cadet for a beautiful landing, they argued, and his next one is worse. Scream
at a cadet for a terrible landing, and his next one is better. Every instructor
in the room had watched it happen.

They were absolutely right about the pattern. The landings really did move that
way. What they had wrong was the cause. A beautiful landing is a good pilot plus
a good day, and the good day does not come back for the next one. Those landings
would have done exactly the same thing if the instructors had stood on the
tarmac and said nothing at all.

## Skill plus luck

Every performance is exactly two things added together: how good you are, and
how the day went.

How good you are is stable. It shows up every time you do. How the day went is
volatile—it is the sudden gust of wind, the lost hour of sleep, the specific
question that happened to be on the exam, or the shot that hits the post instead
of going in.

When you isolate the absolute extremes—the flawless landing, the disastrous
quarter, the team at the very top of the league—you are never just looking at
the most skilled people. You are looking at people whose skill and luck
temporarily pointed in the exact same direction.

The skill stays. The luck evaporates. So when the next measurement inevitably
drifts back toward the middle, it looks for all the world like something
intervened.

But nothing happened to them. You just made a judgment on a number that had luck
built into it, and then watched the luck run out.

## Where this fools people for real

**Medicine.** People go to the doctor when their symptoms are at their absolute
worst. That is almost the definition of deciding to go. So whatever they do
next—a real drug, a herbal tea, a healing crystal—is almost guaranteed to be
followed by improvement. This is not a small psychological effect. It is the
entire reason controlled trials exist. You need a group of people who were
equally miserable and got nothing, or you cannot tell your new treatment from a
normal Tuesday.

**Road safety.** A busy junction has a terrible year for accidents, so the city
installs a traffic camera. The next year has fewer accidents, and the camera
gets all the credit. But the junction was chosen precisely because it had an
unusually bad year, and unusually bad years are followed by ordinary ones all on
their own. Some cameras actually work. You just cannot learn which ones from
that comparison.

**Money.** The mutual fund at the top of the leaderboard this year is there
partly because of skill, but mostly because its wildest bets happened to land
perfectly. Next year, it is ordinary. The leaderboard is not lying to you, and
neither is the manager. You are simply reading a list that was sorted by
skill-plus-luck, and you are mistakenly expecting only the skill part to
persist.

**The peak experience.** Think of the absolute happiest week of your life. The
project launches perfectly, the relationship is electric, the world feels
completely conquered. The human ecstasy of that moment is real, and it tricks
you into thinking you have unlocked a permanent new baseline. But a peak
emotional high is exactly like a flawless airplane landing: it is your baseline
effort plus a massive, temporary spike of luck. It requires every single
volatile variable in your life to line up perfectly at the exact same time. Your
baseline stays, but the luck evaporates. When the following weeks inevitably
feel a little flatter, it isn't because you did something wrong or lost your
edge. The universe just rarely rolls sixes twice in a row.

**Everything with a "curse."** This is the magazine cover jinx, the sophomore
album slump, and the second-season collapse. You get put on the cover of a
magazine precisely because you just hit that peak emotional and professional
high. By the time the issue actually hits the newsstands, the luck has already
evaporated. The inevitable comedown isn't a curse; it is just statistical
gravity.

**Hiring and school.** The most charming interviewer of the day, the student who
topped the entrance exam, the worst-performing employee you threw onto a
mandatory training program. All of them will drift back toward the middle on
their next evaluation. And every time, someone in management will confidently
take the credit or the blame.

## What to do with it

One question does most of the work: **was this picked for being extreme?**

If it was — the top of the leaderboard, the worst-performing team, the patient
at their sickest, the junction with the terrible year — then expect it to drift
toward the middle on its own, and be suspicious of whatever happens to be
standing nearby taking the credit. The only way to know whether the drug, the
camera or the training program actually did anything is to compare it against a
group that was equally extreme and got nothing at all. That is the entire
purpose of a control group.

The same goes for people. Judge them on more than one reading. A single
measurement is skill plus luck; several measurements are mostly skill.

And nothing here is being corrected or repaid. A coin that has come up tails
five times is not due for heads, and a bad quarter does not earn you a good one.
You simply went looking in an unusual spot, and the next look was always going
to be calmer.
