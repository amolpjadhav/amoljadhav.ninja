# TFT Economy: Get So Far Ahead That Losing Is Hard

Category: Gaming

---

Most TFT games are not lost in a fight. They are lost three rounds earlier, at
the shop, by someone spending gold they should have kept.

The version of this game that good players are playing is not "build the
strongest board". It is "get so far ahead of the table that a bad fight cannot
kill me". Those sound the same. They are not. A board that wins this round can
still lose you the game, if paying for it means you arrive at level 8 with 4
gold and no way to roll.

Here is the whole engine.

## The only number you have to remember is 50

At the end of every round you earn interest: **1 gold for every 10 you are
holding, up to 5.**

Read that twice, because two things are hiding in it.

The first is a floor. Below 10 gold, you earn nothing at all. Not a little —
nothing. The first ten gold you save is the hardest to save and pays you back
zero, which is exactly why most people never get past it.

The second is a ceiling. The 50th gold is the last one that earns anything.
Hold 50, earn 5. Hold 90, earn 5. So gold above 50 is *free money* — it earns
nothing by sitting there, which makes it the gold you should be spending.

Between those two numbers, a pile of gold grows faster the bigger it gets. Hold
20 and do nothing at all, and ten rounds later you have 46. You did not play
better. You just did not spend.

## What "hard to lose" actually means

Health is a currency too, and it buys you time. The trap is thinking your job
is to spend health as slowly as possible. Your job is to convert one currency
into the other at a good rate.

Every round you take a loss, you are paying health for gold — the interest that
gold earns, plus the streak bonus for losing, plus an earlier pick at the
carousel, because the lowest-health player chooses first. That is a real
purchase. It is only a bad one if you never spend what you bought.

The player who is hard to beat is not the one at 100 health. It is the one who
arrives at level 8 with 50 gold still in the bank, because they get to look at
more shops than anybody else at the table. Rolling is just buying lottery
tickets, and they turned up with more tickets.

## Pick a streak — either one

Winning pays. Losing pays. Alternating pays nothing.

This is the rule people find hardest, because losing on purpose feels like
losing. It is not: a loss streak is a payment plan. What actually costs you the
game is drifting — winning one, losing one, winning one — collecting the bonus
for neither while your health drains anyway.

So decide early which game you are playing, and commit to it for a few rounds.

## The two ways to spend it

Once the bank is built, there are exactly two things to buy, and every comp in
the [comp sheet](/blog/tft-set-18-comps-all-27-teams-for-enchanted-wilds) is
built around choosing one:

**Levels.** More units on the board, and better odds of the expensive ones
appearing. Fifteen of the 31 comps do this: stop rolling, buy XP, arrive at 8
with gold left, roll once, hard.

**Copies.** Stay at level 6 or 7, where a cheap unit is still common, and spend
only the interest — the gold above 50 — hunting for three copies of it. Fifteen
of the 31 comps do this instead. Nine copies makes a 3-star, which is why it
needs every spare gold and a level you can stop at.

What loses games is doing both at once. Levelling *and* rolling means you never
have enough gold to finish either, and you arrive at stage 5 with a level-8
board of 1-star units.

## When to break the rule

Roll below 50 for exactly two reasons.

**You are one copy away** from the upgrade that wins the round in front of you.

**You will die this round if you don't.** A dead player earns no interest at
all, so the compounding argument stops applying the moment you cannot survive
to collect. This is the part people get backwards: they hold 50 gold at 6 health
and go out in eighth with a lovely bank.

## The version with no ceiling

Here is the thing worth taking out of the game.

That curve — slow at first, faster the more you have, then flat once it hits
the cap — is compound interest. TFT just puts a ceiling on it at 50 gold so a
single lucky player cannot run away with the lobby.

Real compound interest has no ceiling. Same curve, same floor at the start
where it feels pointless, and then it never flattens out. It just keeps bending
upward for as long as you leave it alone.

Thirty minutes of a video game teaches the mechanic. Then reality removes the
cap and runs it for forty years.
