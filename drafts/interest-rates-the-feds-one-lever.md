# Interest Rates: The Fed's One Lever

Category: Economics

---

Twelve people meet in Washington, eight times a year, and decide a single number.

No new laws. No taxes. No spending. Just a number — and within weeks, mortgages get cheaper or pricier, credit card bills swell or shrink, and savings accounts wake up or fall back asleep. For millions of people who will never know those twelve names.

That number is the interest rate. And this is the last piece of a three-part story. Part 1 asked how much money there actually is — the world's cash versus the world's wealth. Part 2 showed [why money keeps losing value](/blog/why-money-loses-value-a-short-history-of-inflation). This one answers the question both of them left hanging: **when money starts melting, what can anyone actually do about it?**

**The one-line idea:** an interest rate is a price tag on borrowing money — and borrowing is how new spending happens. Make borrowing cheap and the economy parties; make it expensive and the party quiets down. The Fed's entire job is picking the volume.

*A note on numbers: the mortgage figures below are exact math for a $400,000, 30-year fixed loan — the same house, only the rate changing. Savings and credit card figures are rounded, typical examples — your bank will differ, but the shape is the same everywhere.*

---

### The thermostat

Your house has a thermostat. Too hot, the AC kicks in. Too cold, the heat comes on. One dial, and the whole house stays livable.

The economy has one too. When everyone is spending furiously, prices surge — that's inflation, the melting money from Part 2. When nobody spends at all, shops close and people lose jobs. The comfortable middle is where the Fed wants to live: prices rising just a touch (about 2% a year — enough to keep things moving, not enough to hurt), and nearly everyone who wants a job able to find one.

The Fed's dial is the interest rate. Turn it **up**, and borrowing gets expensive: fewer mortgages, fewer car loans, fewer business expansions, less spending, cooler prices. Turn it **down**, and borrowing gets cheap: more buying, more hiring, a warmer economy.

That is the whole game. One lever, two directions. Everything below is just that lever reaching into your wallet.

---

### How one number reaches your wallet

The Fed doesn't set your mortgage rate directly. It sets one very specific price: what banks charge *each other* for overnight loans. It's called the federal funds rate, and it's the first domino.

Here's how the dominoes fall:

- **Banks pay more to borrow → they charge you more to borrow.** Mortgages, car loans, and especially credit cards all drift up. A credit card at 22% instead of 18% quietly takes hundreds more from anyone carrying a balance.
- **Banks pay more for deposits → they pay you more to save.** That sleepy savings account earning almost nothing suddenly offers 4% or 5%. For the first time in years, sitting still earns something.
- **Businesses pay more to expand → they open fewer stores and hire fewer people.** The factory waits. The new branch doesn't open. This is the painful half of the lever, and we'll come back to it.

One number ripples into every loan and every savings account in the country within weeks. That's not an exaggeration — it's the plumbing. Money has a price, and the Fed writes the first digit of it.

Now you see why the housing market freezes when rates rise. It's not psychology — it's arithmetic. Millions of buyers who qualified at 3% simply don't qualify at 7%. The music doesn't slow down. It stops.

Borrowers sink. Savers float. And most households are a bit of both — a mortgage on one side, a savings account on the other — so a rate hike squeezes with one hand while feeding with the other.

This is also why "high rates are bad" is only half true. Ask a retiree living off savings interest whether they miss the years their account paid nothing. The lever never helps everyone.

### The price of cool: jobs

Here's the part nobody applauds. Cooling inflation with high rates works — but the medicine has a side effect, and it's people.

When borrowing gets expensive, businesses don't just stop borrowing. They stop expanding, then they start cutting. The new store doesn't open (three jobs never created). Then the quiet store closes (ten jobs destroyed). Every percentage point of cooling lands, eventually, on someone's employment.

The Fed knows this. Its actual legal job is two things at once: **steady prices AND as many people employed as possible.** Economists call it the dual mandate. You can call it the impossible job — because the same lever pushes both goals in opposite directions. Down to fight inflation means up in unemployment, sooner or later. There is no setting that gives you both. There is only picking which problem hurts less right now.

That's worth sitting with. The twelve people aren't wise wizards with a perfect answer. They're stuck holding one lever connected to two things that want opposite settings — and whatever they choose, someone pays.

---

### What the lever can't fix: the broken window

A thermostat keeps a house comfortable. But if a window shatters in January, no thermostat setting fixes the cold. You have to fix the window.

The economy has broken windows too. In 2020, factories shut down and there simply *wasn't enough stuff* — no rate setting manufactures microchips or unclogs ports. When oil spikes because of a war, no interest rate pumps more oil. These are **supply shocks**: prices rising not because too much money is chasing goods, but because the goods vanished.

The lever is nearly useless here — worse, it's the wrong tool entirely. Jacking up rates doesn't print a single microchip; it just adds expensive borrowing on top of empty shelves. Economists have a bleak phrase for it: *pushing on a string.* You can pull spending down, but you can't push goods into existence.

So the honest boundary is this: the Fed can fix too-much-money problems. It cannot fix not-enough-stuff problems. Knowing which kind of inflation you're looking at is half of understanding the news — and now you can tell them apart.

### What to actually do when rates move

The whole article compresses to five rules:

1. **When rates are high, kill variable-rate debt first.** Credit cards reset upward almost immediately. A balance you could lazily carry at low rates becomes an emergency at 22%. Pay it like the house is on fire — because mathematically, it is.
2. **When rates are high, finally care about your savings account.** The gap between a lazy 0.1% account and a 4.5% one is hundreds of dollars a year on real balances. High rates are the one moment shopping for yield is worth an afternoon.
3. **When rates are low, that's the window for big fixed borrowing.** Mortgages, refinancing, starting the business — low rates are a sale on money itself. It doesn't last; the thermostat always turns again.
4. **Never assume today's rate is normal.** People who bought houses at 3% treated it as the weather. It was the sale. Every rate in history felt permanent and wasn't.
5. **Read every Fed headline as a seesaw, not a verdict.** "Fed raises rates" doesn't mean good or bad — it means borrowers just lost and savers just won, and someone chose melting money over expensive borrowing. Now you know enough to ask who, and why.

## The one thing to remember

Prices, jobs, mortgages, savings — they look like separate stories, but they're one machine with one dial.

**Borrowing has a price, spending follows the price, and twelve people set the price eight times a year.** Cheap money throws a party and melts the currency. Expensive money ends the party and costs jobs. There is no setting where nobody pays — there's only choosing *who* pays, *when*.

That's the end of the trilogy. Part 1 counted the money. Part 2 watched it melt. Part 3 met the people holding the thermostat — and now you know exactly what their one lever does, who it helps, who it hurts, and what it can never fix.
