# The Italian Game: Your First Chess Opening, Move by Move

Category: Gaming

---

Every chess player learns the same first opening, and it is four hundred years old. It starts 1. e4 e5 2. Nf3 Nc6 3. Bc4 — three moves s remarkably little, and teaches nearly everything: control the center, develop your pieces, guard your king, and give every move a job.

This is the Italian Game, and this article walks through it the slow way: what each move means, what each side is threatening, and the two famous roads Black can take at move three. No prior opening knowledge assumed. If you can read chess notation (e4 is a square, Nf3 is a knight moving to f3, O-O means castling), you are ready.

One square runs the whole show, so learn it first: **f7**. It is the weakest square on Black's side — the only square near the Black king defended by just the king himself. White's entire opening is a long stare at f7. (Mirror image: f2 is White's weak square, and Black stares right back.)

### The fundamentals hiding in three moves

Beginners sometimes hear opening advice as vague proverbs. Watch how the first three moves *are* the proverbs:

**Control the center.** Pawns on e4 and e5 grab the middle squares, and everything important — development, attacks, space — flows from there. Edge pawns (like h4 on move one) control nothing and free nothing, which is why strong players almost never start with them.

**Develop knights before bishops, and minor pieces before the queen.** Knights have only one good direction — toward the center — so move them first. Bishops need open diagonals, which pawns must clear first. And the queen? She is your strongest piece, which makes her a target: bring her out early and your opponent develops pieces *by chasing her around*. Notice neither queen has moved in our line.

**Castle early.** Every developing move in the Italian points at one goal: get the king safe behind pawns and connect the rooks. White's Nf3 and Bc4 both clear pieces out from between the king and the corner. Within a few more moves, White will play O-O and the king disappears to safety.

**Every move should do something.** 1. e4 claims and frees. 2. Nf3 develops, prepares castling, *and* attacks. 2...Nc6 defends *and* develops. If your move neither develops, threatens, nor fixes a problem, suspect it.

### Road one: the quiet game (3...Bc5)

Black's most classical reply develops the other bishop to mirror White: the Giuoco Piano, Italian for "quiet game." Don't let the name fool you — it is quiet the way a coiled spring is quiet. Both sides castle fast, and then White winds up the central break both sides have been preparing: pawns to c3 and d4, seizing the whole center at once.

The typical flow: 3...Bc5 develops with an eye on f2. White plays 4. c3 — a humble pawn step with a big idea: support the coming d4 push. Black develops with 4...Nf6, attacking e4 the same way White attacked e5. White holds everything together with 5. d3, defending the e-pawn while keeping the d4 break loaded for later. Black castles, safe and connected. The position looks symmetrical and sleepy — and one d4 later, the center explodes open. Whoever understands the break better usually wins it.

The lesson of this road: **openings are arguments about the center, conducted in slow motion.** c3 looks like nothing. It is the foundation d4 stands on.

### Road two: the wild game (3...Nf6)

Instead of mirroring, Black can punch back: 3...Nf6 attacks White's e4 pawn and opens the Two Knights Defense, the fighting cousin of the quiet game. Now White faces the first real decision of the opening — and beginners get their first famous trap.

White's sharpest answer is 4. Ng5, piling a second attacker onto poor f7 (remember: defended by the king alone). Black's correct response is the calm central punch 4...d5, striking back instead of panicking. After 5. exd5, Black must retreat the attacked knight with 5...Na5 — hitting White's c4 bishop and accepting an awkward but playable position.

But suppose Black gets greedy and recaptures with 5...Nxd5 instead. Then comes the most famous punishment in beginner chess: **6. Nxf7!** White sacrifices the knight, drags the Black king into the middle of the board, and launches an attack that wins by force with best play. It even has a name — the Fried Liver Attack — because, as an old Italian master put it, the Black king's insides are all cut up. If you take one thing from this road: *when your opponent attacks f7 twice, do not grab pawns. Hit back in the center.*

### Mistakes every beginner makes (in this exact opening)

**Playing for Scholar's mate.** After 1. e4 e5 2. Bc4?! Nc6 3. Qh5?!, White threatens Qxf7 mate in one — and any player who has seen it once plays 3...g6 or 3...Qe7 and suddenly White's queen is a refugee. The Italian teaches the antidote: develop, castle, *then* attack f7 with pieces, not an early queen.

**Grabbing the e-pawn's defenders away.** Moves like 4. Nxe5?? (after 3...Nf6) win a pawn and lose the game: Black plays 4...Nxe4 or Qe7 and White's center collapses. Pawns you have to defend with three pieces are not free.

**Forgetting Black has the same ideas.** Every f7 threat White dreams of, Black dreams mirrored on f2. The Giuoco Piano bishop on c5 is not decoration — step into a tactic blindness and ...Bxf2+ ruins your castled afternoon.

**Memorizing past move five.** Club players win the most games not by knowing move twelve, but by understanding moves one to five and then playing principled chess: develop, castle, central breaks, no early queen, no greed. The steppers above are the part worth memorizing. Everything after is thinking.

---

## The one thing to remember

The Italian Game is three ideas wearing a trench coat: own the center, bring out your pieces toward it, and lock the king away before the fighting starts. Learn what 1. e4, 2. Nf3, and 3. Bc4 each *do*, pick your road at move three, and you already understand more opening theory than most casual players ever will.
