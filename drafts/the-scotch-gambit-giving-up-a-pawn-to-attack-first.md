# The Scotch Gambit: Giving Up a Pawn to Attack First

Here's a chess opening that starts with White giving away a pawn on purpose:

1. e4 e5
2. Nf3 Nc6
3. d4 exd4
4. Bc4

After 3...exd4, White could just take the pawn straight back with the knight. Instead, White plays a bishop move and leaves the pawn sitting there for Black to keep. Why would you hand your opponent a free pawn?

---

### What you're actually trading for

White is trading material for speed. Every move in this opening develops a piece and points it at Black's position, while Black has to decide what to do with an extra pawn that isn't doing much except sitting there. By the time Black's done deciding, White is already further ahead in the race to get pieces out and ready to attack.

A pawn is worth something. But so is time — and falling behind in development can cost a lot more than one pawn is worth.

### The target: f7

Look at where that bishop on c4 is pointing: straight down the diagonal at f7, the pawn in front of Black's king.

f7 is special. At the start of the game, only one piece defends it — the king. Every other pawn is backed up by a piece that can actually afford to recapture if needed: a rook, a bishop, the queen. f7's only defender is the king, and the king can't safely march forward into a capture. Worse, moving the king at all — even just to grab a pawn — means giving up castling for the rest of the game, the move that tucks the king into a safe corner and brings a rook into play. That makes f7 the weakest point in Black's position in the opening, and the Scotch Gambit's whole plan is built around attacking it before Black gets time to protect it properly.

---

### Common traps

There are two traps that come up most often here. Both branch off right after 4. Bc4, when Black makes a different choice on move 4 and reaches for more material instead of catching up on development. Step through each one and watch what happens.

**Trap 1: the premature check**

Instead of developing normally, Black opens with a check: 4...Bb4+. White blocks it with c3, which also attacks the bishop — and Black grabs the pawn on c3 instead of saving the bishop. That's the mistake. By the time the bishop retreats to safety, White's queen and bishop are lined up on the same diagonal, threatening mate on f7.

**Trap 2: the bishop sacrifice**

This one starts the same way, but Black develops the bishop normally instead of checking. The greed shows up a move later: on move 5, Black grabs a third pawn instead of catching up on development. White answers by sacrificing the bishop on f7, dragging the king out into the open, then forks the king and the bishop to win the material straight back — with interest.

The pawns in both traps were never really free. Every move Black spent grabbing more material was a move not spent developing, and the Scotch Gambit is built entirely around making that trade-off expensive.

### How Black can counter it

So far, Black has been cooperating by grabbing extra pawns. A well-prepared opponent doesn't. Instead of taking more material, the soundest reply counter-attacks the center immediately:

By move 9, Black is fully developed, material is even, and the position is balanced. That's what happens against someone who actually knows this line — the gambit doesn't win anything by itself. It just leads to a normal, roughly equal game.

---

### Who should play it

The Scotch Gambit rewards players who like to attack. It's sharp, it's fast, and it punishes an opponent who isn't paying attention to development. At club level, where most games aren't perfectly precise, that makes it a genuinely dangerous weapon.

It's less effective against a well-prepared opponent who knows the lines and develops calmly — against a player like that, giving up a pawn for an attack that gets neutralized just means you're down a pawn. Like most gambits, it's a bet that your opponent won't defend perfectly. Against most humans, most of the time, that's a good bet.
