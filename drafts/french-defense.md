# The French Defense: Every Line, Move by Move

Category: Gaming

---

After 1. e4, Black usually mirrors with 1...e5 and accepts an open duel. The French player refuses. With 1...e6, Black says: take the center — I dare you — and then strikes at it with 2...d5. What follows is the most counterpunching opening in chess: White builds a proud pawn center, Black spends the whole game undermining it with ...c5 and ...f6, and somebody's plan breaks first.

This article walks every road after 1. e4 e6 2. d4 d5: the four moves White can try on move three, Black's answers to each, and the traps hiding along the way. If you can read chess notation (e4 is a square, Nf3 is a knight moving to f3, O-O means castling), you are ready.

Two ideas run the whole show, so learn them first. **The pawn chain.** After 1. e4 e6 2. d4 d5, pawns lock on e6-d5 for Black and e4-d4 for White. Chains are attacked at their base: White's base is d4 (hit it with ...c5), Black's base is e6 (hit it with f4-f5 or pressure down the e-file). Nearly every French line is an argument about the d4-pawn. **The problem child.** Black's light-squared bishop on c8 starts buried behind its own e6-pawn. All of Black's plans must either free it (...b6 and ...Bb7, or trading it with ...Bd7/....Ba4) or accept it and play around it. When you wonder why Black made some strange queenside move, the answer is usually the c8-bishop.

### The fork at move three: four moves, four worlds

White has four serious tries, and each leads to a different kind of game:

**3. Nc3** develops and defends e4 — the principled main line, and Black's most testing reply is the pin 3...Bb4 (the Winawer). Quieter Blacks play 3...Nf6 (the Classical) or give up the center at once with 3...dxe4 (the Rubinstein).

**3. Nd2** (the Tarrasch) also defends e4 but keeps the c-pawn's path clear and avoids the Winawer pin entirely. Solid, flexible, slightly less ambitious.

**3. e5** (the Advance) grabs space and locks the center shut. White says: my chain against yours — try to break it.

**3. exd5** (the Exchange) releases all the tension on move three. Simple, symmetrical, and the drawing weapon of choice.

Everything below is one of these four worlds. Pick the one that matches your temperament — or learn them all and never fear 1...e6 again.

### Road one: the Winawer (3...Bb4)

The most feared answer to 3. Nc3: Black pins the knight that defends e4, and threatens to take it and ruin White's pawns. White's normal reply is the space grab 4. e5, Black undermines the base at once with 4...c5, and after 5. a3 Black usually accepts the doubled pawns with 5...Bxc3+ 6. bxc3. White gets the bishop pair and attacking chances against the Black king; Black gets a rock-solid center and a queenside pawn majority for the endgame. Both sides are playing for a win.

The typical flow: 6...Ne7 develops while keeping ...Nbc6 and ...Qc7 options open. White develops 7. Nf3, Black continues 7...Nbc6 pressuring d4 and e5. From here White castles and plays for f4-f5 or the kingside, Black plays ...Qc7, ...cxd4 at the right moment, and ...b6 to free the problem bishop. Deep, sharp, and argued about for a century.

The daring alternative is 6...Qc7!?, keeping queens on and eyeing both the c3-pawn and kingside complications — the infamous **Poisoned Pawn**. White often replies 7. Qg4, staring at g7. Black grabs material and dares White to prove the attack. Only for the brave, on both sides.

### Winawer sidelines: three ways to dodge the main road

Not everyone wants the doubled pawns. Three sidelines matter:

**The Armenian (5...Ba5).** Black keeps the bishop instead of trading it. White's refutation is instant and instructive: 6. b4! kicks it again. Every bishop retreat is a move Black doesn't spend developing — and White gains time with every kick. Play 5...Ba5 and you spend the opening running. That is why Black normally takes on c3.

**The exchange (4. exd5).** White releases the tension at once: 4...exd5 and the center opens. Black develops naturally (...Nf6, ...Bd6 or ...Be7) and White gets a tiny lead in development — and nothing else. Safe for White, comfortable for Black, and a fine choice if you know the middlegame better than your opponent.

**The knight retreat (4. Nge2).** White's cleverest dodge: develop the kingside knight *behind* the center so that after 4...dxe4, 5. a3 attacks a bishop with no good capture — 5...Be7 ducks all the way home, refusing the doubled-pawn trade Black wanted. White keeps a clean pawn structure; Black keeps the bishop pair's absence as compensation. The lesson: move order is a weapon — the same bishop behaves differently depending on when you ask it questions.

Two more Winawer tries deserve one line each. **4. a3?!** too early is the beginner's trap: 4...Bxc3+ 5. bxc3 dxe4 wins the e-pawn, because the knight that defended it is gone. **4. Bd2** simply unpins and keeps pieces on — Black continues 4...c5 or 4...Ne7 with a normal game. Principles beat memorization: develop, don't hang e4, ask questions with pawns.

### Road two: the Classical (3...Nf6)

No pin, no tricks: Black develops the kingside knight and attacks e4 the honest way. White almost always pushes past with 4. e5, and the knight must retreat — 4...Nfd7 keeps both central breaks (...c5 and ...f6) alive. Then comes the signature move of the whole variation: 5. f4, defending e5 with another pawn and building the massive center White dreams about.

Black's answer never changes: 5...c5 hits the base again, White develops 6. Nf3, Black develops 6...Nc6 — and 7. Be3 defends d4 while eyeing the queenside. This is the Steinitz Variation: maximum tension, both chains intact, both breaks loaded. White has space; Black has targets. The player who understands pawn breaks wins it.

White's ambitious alternative is **4. Bg5**, pinning before pushing — met by the cold shower 4...dxe4!, opening the center while the bishop stands misplaced on g5. And the quiet **4. ### Road three: the Rubinstein (3...dxe4)

Black can skip the philosophy and trade the center at once: 3...dxe4 4. Nxe4, and develop around it — 4...Nd7, 5. Nf3 Ngf6, 6. Nxf6+ Nxf6. The position looks almost symmetrical, and that is exactly the point: Black accepts a slightly passive but nearly unbreakable game. The famous setup even has a fortress name — the **Fort Knox**: ...Be7 (or ...Bd6), ...Ne7, ...c6, ...O-O, nothing hanging anywhere, good luck breaking in.

White's edge is real but thin: better development and chances to press. This is the road for Black players who would rather defend one good position for forty moves than memorize Winawer theory for four hours.

### Road four: the Tarrasch (3. Nd2)

White's classy sidestep: defend e4 with the queen's knight, keeping the kingside knight home and — critically — making 3...Bb4 pointless (no knight on c3 to pin). Against it, Black's most reliable answer is the closed setup 3...Nf6: White pushes 4. e5, Black retreats 4...Nfd7, White solidifies with 5. Bd3, Black hits the base with 5...c5, White props it with 6. c3, Black develops 6...Nc6. Same pawn-chain war as the Classical, one file over: White's center is safer, Black's breaks still come.

Three Black alternatives every Tarrasch player must know. **The open Tarrasch (3...c5)** strikes the d-pawn sideways at once: 4. exd5 Qxd5! (recapturing with the queen — 4...exd5 leaves Black an isolated queen's pawn to nurse all game). **3...dxe4** trades like the Rubinstein and develops the same way. And **the Guimard (3...Nc6)** develops with tempo on d4: 4. Ngf3 Nf6 5. e5 Nd7 reaches a closed position where Black's knight already eyes the center. Different move orders, same war.

### Road five: the Advance (3. e5)

White locks the center and says: break me if you can. Black's only serious answer is 3...c5 — same base, same hammer. White props it with 4. c3, Black develops 4...Nc6, White develops 5. Nf3, and Black brings the queen out early with 5...Qb6, tripling the pressure on d4 (queen, knight, and the c5-pawn all stare at it) while eyeing b2 on the side.

Then comes the star move: 6. a3! threatens b4, grabbing queenside space and challenging the c5-pawn's whole existence. Black's modern answer is the knight dance 6...Nh6!? — heading for f5, the perfect blockading square in front of White's chain. The Advance is a slow squeeze from both sides: White pushes b4 and f4-f5, Black pushes ...cxd4 and ...f6. Whoever's break lands first usually wins.

The old alternative 5...Bd7 just develops and waits — solid but passive, and White squeezes with 6. a3 anyway. In the Advance, ...c5 first, questions later.

### Road six: the Exchange (3. exd5)

The peacemaker's road: 3. exd5 exd5 and every drop of tension leaves the position. Both sides develop identically — 4. Nf3, 4...Bd6, 5. Bd3 — and the game becomes about who plays the middlegame better with zero opening edge to argue over. White keeps the move-first straw to clutch; Black has nothing to fear and nothing to attack. Club wisdom: play this when you'd rather outplay than outprepare.

### The side doors: move-two and move-three oddities

A few tries don't fit the six roads but show up enough to recognize:

**2. d3 (the King's Indian Attack setup).** White refuses the center fight and builds a fortress: ...d5 3. Nd2 Nf6 4. Ngf3, then g3, Bg2, O-O, e5 at the right moment. No theory, all understanding — and Black must be careful not to overextend against it.

**2. Qe2 (the Chigorin).** The queen defends e4 while keeping every pawn tension alive. Solid and slightly passive; Black develops normally with ...d5 (yes, still) and ...Nf6.

**2. Nf3 d5 3. e5.** White grabs space a move early — but without d4 supporting it, Black hits ...c5 with extra effect and develops with tempo. Space without support is a weakness wearing a costume.

**3. Bd3?!** blocks the d-pawn with its own bishop — the single most common beginner mistake in the French. Your d-pawn is your center; don't sit on it. **3. f4?!** weakens e4 and the kingside for zero reason. **3. Qg4?!** brings the queen out to stare at g7 — and after 3...Nf6! the queen must run while Black develops. The queen is a target, not an explorer.

### Mistakes every beginner makes (in this exact opening)

**Asking with 4. a3?! before pushing e5.** The Winawer trap from the sidelines section: 4...Bxc3+ 5. bxc3 dxe4 wins a pawn, because the knight that defended e4 is sitting in a captured-pieces box. Push e5 first, ask questions second.

**Playing 3. Bd3 and sitting on your own center.** The bishop looks developed. It is a roadblock: the d-pawn can't move, e4 loses its conveniences, and Black plays ...c5 with a free hand. Develop knights first — they don't block anything.

**Releasing the tension early as Black.** Moves like ...cxd4 or ...dxe4 (outside the Rubinstein) feel freeing — and hand White open lines and development tempo for nothing. The French rewards patience: keep ...c5 and ...f6 loaded until the break wins material or freedom, not just fresh air.

**Memorizing past move eight.** Club games in the French are won by understanding the chain — hit the base, prop the base, free the c8-bishop — not by reciting move fourteen of the Winawer. The steppers above are the part worth memorizing. Everything after is thinking.

---

## The one thing to remember

The French Defense is one idea wearing six coats: White builds a pawn chain, Black spends the whole game hitting its base with ...c5 and ...f6 while solving the c8-bishop. Learn what d4 means to White and what c5 means to Black, pick the road that matches your nerve, and 1...e6 stops being scary — it starts being fun.
