# Why Birds Can Sit on Power Lines (and You Can't)

Category: Science

---

A bird sits on a power line carrying thousands of volts, completely relaxed. Grab that same wire and it would kill you. Same wire, same voltage, so what's actually different about the two of you?

The answer fits in one sentence: voltage is a difference, and the bird has none across its body. Difference between what? That's where it gets interesting, and it starts with the ground under your feet.

## 12,000 volts compared to what?

When the power company says a line is "12,000 volts," that's an incomplete sentence. 12,000 volts *compared to what?*

Think of voltage like water pressure and current like flow. A tank 100 feet up pushes water down hard — 100 feet *above the ground*. The ground is the silent second half of every pressure reading. Now put a second tank right next to the first one, also 100 feet up, and connect the two with a pipe. Nothing flows. Both sides push equally. Enormous pressure, zero flow — because flow only ever cares about the *difference*.

That's exactly how voltage works, and "the ground" is literally called ground: electricians treat the earth as zero volts, so 120 in the wall means 120 volts above ground. Pole voltages are usually quoted wire-to-wire: a "12,000-volt" neighborhood line is about 7,200 volts above ground. And it's not just a convention: the power company literally wires its system to the earth. Copper runs from every pole transformer down into a rod buried in the soil, so the ground under you is connected to the other end of the circuit. Touch a live wire while standing on it and the current finally has a path home — through you.

But ground is just one possible second point. Measure between two live wires instead and you get the *wire-to-wire* voltage, which can be completely different — or, if both wires sit at the same voltage, zero.

So the bird's question was never "how many volts is the wire?" It was always "how many volts *between the things I'm touching*?" Both feet on one wire: same voltage twice, difference zero. That's the whole trick, and now you can go play with it:

## Electricity only flows downhill

Voltage is a push, and a push only moves things from high to low. Same voltage on both sides of you? Nothing moves, no matter how high it is.

A bird standing on one wire has both feet at the same voltage. The wire might be thousands of volts relative to the ground, but the bird isn't touching the ground. Across the bird's own body, the difference is essentially zero. Zero difference, zero push, zero current. The bird could be sitting at a million volts and physics wouldn't care — as long as *all of it* is at a million volts.

## The two ways to die

Flip through the scenarios and notice the pattern. Every zap has the same shape: **two contact points at two different voltages, with your body bridging them.**

- The bird touching two wires: feet on one wire, head brushing another. Different wires, different voltages, bird in the middle.
- You grabbing a wire: hand at about 7,200 volts, feet on the ground at zero. You are the bridge between the wire and the earth.

And every safe case has the opposite shape: everything you touch is at the *same* voltage. The bird on one wire. The lineman on his insulated platform — he works on live wires all day, and the platform is what makes him a bird: no path to anywhere else, so no current.

There's a size limit to the trick, and eagles found it. A songbird fits on one wire touching nothing else, but an eagle's six-foot wingspan can brush two wires at once — or a wire and the grounded metal pole. That's why poles in raptor country wear chunky insulated covers with extra-wide wire spacing: "raptor-safe" hardware keeps big wings from becoming bridges. Squirrels show up in outage reports for the same reason — scamper across a transformer, touch a live bushing and the grounded case at once, and the squirrel is the circuit.

## How much current is too much?

The widget uses 100,000 ohms for dry human skin, which is roughly right. A person standing on the ground touching a 7,200-volt wire: about 72 milliamps through the body — already in the danger zone below. And that's optimistic — at thousands of volts skin breaks down and its resistance collapses, so the real current is far higher. Here's the scale that number sits on:

- Below about 1 milliamp: you feel nothing.
- Around 10 milliamps: muscles lock and you can't let go.
- Around 50–100 milliamps: enough to throw the heart out of rhythm.

So a wall socket at 120 volts through dry skin gives about 1 milliamp — a tingle. The same socket with wet hands (skin resistance can drop toward 1,000 ohms) gives 120 milliamps — lethal. It's never the volts alone. It's volts *across you*, divided by your resistance.

## So could you sit on a wire?

In theory, yes — and some people do it for a living. Bare-hand linemen work on live high-voltage lines from helicopters and insulated buckets: they bond themselves to the wire with a metal wand first, so there's never a difference between them and the line to push current, and they wear a conductive suit that keeps every inch of them at exactly line voltage. Up there, the lineman is the bird — everything he touches is the same volts. The helicopter, the bucket, the suit: all of it exists to guarantee a single point of contact with the world. One stray touch of ground and the trick ends.

For everyone else, it's fatal, for reasons the bird never faces. You can't reach a wire without touching something on the way — a ladder, a tree, the pole. The first touch arcs as your body jumps from zero to thousands of volts; that's what the lineman's wand is for. And you're six feet wide; the next wire or the grounded crossarm is often within reach. The bird is safe because it's small and it flew there. You're neither.

## The footnote that's technically true

Sharp readers will object: the bird's two feet aren't at *exactly* the same point on the wire, and wire has a tiny bit of resistance, so there must be a *tiny* voltage difference between its feet. Correct — a few millivolts, pushing a fraction of a microamp — thousands of times below anything you could feel. You would need laboratory equipment to notice, and the bird needs nothing at all. Zero-ish is zero for all practical purposes.

## The takeaway

Electricity doesn't hunt you down for touching something "high voltage." It only flows downhill, from one voltage to a lower one, through whatever connects them. Don't be the connection.

One real-life rule before you go: treat every wire as live. A downed line after a storm, a ladder tipping back into the wires over your house, even a kite string draped across a wire — any of them can make you the bridge. Give lines plenty of space and call the utility; never assume a wire is dead just because it looks quiet.
